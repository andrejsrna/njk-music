Background music can make a gaming video feel faster, cleaner, funnier, darker, more cinematic, or more professional. The right track gives your edit momentum. The wrong track fights the gameplay, distracts from commentary, or creates copyright problems after upload.

If you create gameplay videos, stream highlights, YouTube Shorts, Twitch clips, esports edits, walkthroughs, or game trailers, choosing music is not just a taste decision. It affects pacing, viewer retention, monetization, and whether the video stays online without muted sections or claims.

This guide explains how to choose the best background music for gaming videos and how to keep your soundtrack creator-safe.

## 1. Why Background Music Matters in Gaming Videos

Gaming videos already have a lot happening at once: gameplay audio, voice chat, narration, UI sounds, explosions, menu clicks, and sometimes stream alerts. Background music should support that energy instead of competing with it.

Good gaming background music does three things:

- **Sets the mood** before the viewer fully understands the scene
- **Keeps momentum** during transitions, cuts, menus, and slower gameplay moments
- **Makes the edit feel intentional** instead of leaving raw gameplay audio to carry everything

For example, a fast phonk or drum and bass track can make a kill montage feel aggressive and high-impact. A lofi beat can make a cozy building game, Minecraft session, or late-night stream recap feel relaxed. A cinematic electronic cue can make a boss fight or trailer-style edit feel bigger than the raw footage.

The goal is not to make the music louder than the game. The goal is to make the viewer feel the video more clearly.

## 2. Match the Music to the Game Genre

The easiest way to choose music is to start with the game’s emotional shape. A horror game, racing game, tactical shooter, farming sim, and RPG walkthrough all need different musical energy.

Here are a few practical matches:

| Video type | Music direction | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FPS highlights | Phonk, trap, EDM, hard rock, aggressive DnB | Adds impact, speed, and confidence |
| Racing or action gameplay | Drum and bass, synthwave, techno | Keeps forward motion and intensity |
| Strategy or tutorial videos | Lofi, chillhop, ambient electronic | Supports concentration without distracting |
| Cozy games and building games | Soft lofi, acoustic textures, warm chill beats | Keeps the video relaxed and friendly |
| Horror edits | Dark ambient, minimal cinematic pulses | Builds tension without overpowering jump scares |
| Esports trailers | Hybrid electronic, cinematic drums, bass-heavy cues | Makes the edit feel competitive and polished |

If the game already has strong in-game music, use background tracks carefully. You may only need music during the intro, montage section, or outro. If the video is mostly commentary, choose instrumental music with fewer melodic hooks so the voice stays clear.

## 3. Best Music Genres for Gameplay, Montages, and Streams

Different gaming formats need different levels of intensity. A montage can handle a track that dominates the edit. A tutorial usually cannot.

### High-energy montages

For frag movies, clutch moments, racing edits, and fast cuts, choose music with clear drops, strong drums, and obvious section changes. Genres like DnB, phonk, trap, EDM, and rock work well because the beat gives you natural edit points.

### Streams and long-form gameplay

Streams need music that can run for longer without exhausting the viewer. Avoid tracks with constant drops, loud vocal chops, or sudden dramatic breaks unless the stream is built around high-energy moments.

### Tutorials, reviews, and commentary

For videos where the voice matters most, music should stay simple. Look for instrumental tracks with steady rhythm, minimal vocals, and a clean mix. If a beat is too busy, viewers may struggle to follow the explanation.

A good rule: if the music makes you raise your voice while editing, it is probably too much for a tutorial.

## 4. How to Avoid Copyright Claims on YouTube and Twitch

The best gaming background music is not only the track that sounds good. It also needs to be safe for the platform where you publish.

YouTube, Twitch, Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok all have systems that can detect copyrighted music. If a track is not properly licensed, your video may be muted, blocked, demonetized, or claimed by a rights holder.

To reduce risk:

1. **Use music from an official source.** Download from the artist, label, platform, or library that actually owns or controls the track.
2. **Read the license before publishing.** “No copyright” can mean different things depending on the creator.
3. **Keep proof of permission.** Save receipts, license pages, download records, or screenshots.
4. **Avoid random reuploads.** A song uploaded by an unknown channel may not be cleared even if the title says “copyright free.”
5. **Check attribution rules.** Some tracks require credit in the video description.

## 7. Quick Checklist Before Publishing Your Video

Before uploading your gaming video, run through this checklist:

- Does the music match the pace of the gameplay?
- Is the music low enough under commentary and important game audio?
- Does the track have a clear license for YouTube, Twitch, or your target platform?
- Do you need attribution in the video description?
- Have you saved proof of permission or the source page?
- Does the music loop cleanly if the video is long?
- Are the intro, drop, and ending aligned with the edit?

Small checks like these prevent avoidable problems later. They also make your video feel more polished because the music supports the structure instead of sitting randomly underneath it.

## 8. FAQ

### What is the best background music for gaming videos?

The best background music depends on the video format. Montages usually need energetic genres like DnB, phonk, EDM, trap, or rock. Tutorials and commentary videos usually work better with lofi, chillhop, ambient electronic, or subtle hip hop instrumentals.

### Can I use no copyright music on monetized YouTube videos?

Often yes, but only if the license allows commercial or monetized use. Always read the license terms from the original source and keep proof of permission.

### Is royalty-free music the same as no copyright music?

Not exactly. Royalty-free usually means you can use the track without paying recurring royalties after the license is granted. It does not mean the track has no copyright. No copyright music is often used informally, so the license still matters.

### What volume should background music be in a gaming video?

There is no universal number, but the voice and important gameplay sounds should stay clear. If the viewer has to fight the music to understand commentary, lower it. For montage sections with no voice, the music can be more present.
